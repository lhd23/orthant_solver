\begin{abstract}
The Gaussian probability solver evaluates rectangular events by three routes:
a closed differential system for integrals over the faces of a box, an
auxiliary-field integral for exchangeable precision matrices, and a
conditional common-factor integral for equal nonnegative correlations.
This document derives the reductions, explains how their numerical evaluation
uses BootLoops, and identifies the exact symbolic operations performed by
SymPy. It also describes the precision checks and the optional compiled
arithmetic. The derivations document the current source; the recently added
performance changes have not been executed or validated. Existing numerical
receipts belong to the earlier implementation.
\end{abstract}

\section{The probability and its relation to integral transport}

Let $X$ have a $d$-dimensional normal distribution with mean $\mu$ and
positive-definite covariance matrix $\Sigma$. Write $Q=\Sigma^{-1}$ for
its precision matrix. After centering, the bounds become
$\ell_i=L_i-\mu_i$ and $u_i=U_i-\mu_i$. The quantity computed is
\begin{equation}
 p=\Prob(L\leq X\leq U)
 =\frac{\sqrt{\det Q}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}
   \int_{\ell\leq x\leq u}\exp\!\left(-\frac12x^{\mathsf T}Qx\right)\dd^dx .
 \label{eq:probability}
\end{equation}
The inequalities are componentwise. A positive orthant has $L_i=0$ and
$U_i=+\infty$; arbitrary finite or infinite endpoints give a box event.
The public functions accept either a covariance matrix or a precision matrix,
and return both $p$ and its natural logarithm.

The general algorithm follows the integration route described in Section 3.2
of the supplied BootLoops paper~\cite{bootloops}: choose a family of
integrals, reduce parameter derivatives to a finite set of master integrals,
and solve their differential equations from a known boundary value.
Integration-by-parts reduction and differential equations are established
methods for Feynman integrals~\cite{chetyrkin,kotikov}.
The auxiliary mass flow package of Liu and Ma~\cite{amflow}, cited at
\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11669}{arXiv:2201.11669}, is a particularly
clear example of this strategy: an auxiliary mass parameter connects a
tractable limiting problem to the desired integral through a rational
differential system. In the present Gaussian problem, the continuation
parameter changes correlations and the starting point consists of independent
coordinates. No auxiliary mass parameter is introduced, and the executable
solver does not call the auxiliary mass flow package.

There is also directly relevant statistical literature: Koyama and Takemura
evaluate Gaussian orthant probabilities through a holonomic differential
system~\cite{holonomic}. The face-integral construction below belongs to
this same broad class of finite differential reductions. It is a Gaussian
adaptation using BootLoops numerical engines, rather than a claim that
scattering-amplitude identities transfer to a Gaussian integrand unchanged.
In particular, the code does not construct a scattering alphabet, fit a
polylogarithmic function space, or recognize constants through integer
relations.

\section{Closing the Gaussian family under differentiation}

\subsection{A positive-definite path with an independent starting point}

Define $D=\operatorname{diag}(Q_{11},\ldots,Q_{dd})$ and $E=Q-D$.
The implemented deformation is $Q(t)=D+tE$, with $0\leq t\leq1$.
Thus $Q(0)=D$ and $Q(1)=Q$. Every intermediate matrix is positive definite:
for nonzero $v$, the quadratic form
$v^{\mathsf T}Q(t)v=(1-t)v^{\mathsf T}Dv+t v^{\mathsf T}Qv$ is positive.
This gives an admissible real continuation path with an elementary
independent-coordinate boundary condition.

\subsection{Face integrals are the master integrals}

A face $F$ specifies which coordinates are free, fixed at a finite lower
endpoint, or fixed at a finite upper endpoint. Let $A$ be its free-coordinate
set, $B$ its fixed-coordinate set, and $b$ the vector of fixed values. Its
unnormalized master integral is
\begin{equation}
 J_F(t)=\int_{\ell_A\leq x_A\leq u_A}
 \exp\!\left[-\frac12
 \begin{pmatrix}x_A\\b\end{pmatrix}^{\!\mathsf T}
 \begin{pmatrix}Q_{AA}(t)&Q_{AB}(t)\\Q_{BA}(t)&Q_{BB}(t)\end{pmatrix}
 \begin{pmatrix}x_A\\b\end{pmatrix}\right]\dd x_A .
 \label{eq:face}
\end{equation}
When no coordinates are free, this is simply the Gaussian exponential
evaluated at a corner. The full-box integral is $J_{\varnothing}$.
Infinite endpoints do not create fixed faces: their Gaussian boundary
contributions vanish.

Differentiating $J_F$ with respect to $t$ produces first and second moments
over the same face. For free indices, define
$m_i=\int_F x_i f_t\,\dd x_A$ and
$M_{ij}=\int_F x_i x_j f_t\,\dd x_A$, where $f_t$ is the exponential
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:face}. These moments can be eliminated without adding
new master integrals.

\subsection{Integration by parts, including the boundary terms}

Set $R=Q_{AA}(t)^{-1}$ and $c=Q_{AB}(t)b$. For a free coordinate $j$,
let $F_j^+$ and $F_j^-$ be its upper and lower child faces, omitting an
infinite endpoint. Put $\Delta_j=J_{F_j^+}-J_{F_j^-}$, with a missing
face contributing zero. Integrating $\partial f_t/\partial x_j$ gives
$\Delta_j=-\sum_k Q_{jk}(t)m_k-c_jJ_F$.

For the second moment, let $H_{ji}$ be the upper-minus-lower boundary
integral of $x_i f_t$ on coordinate $j$. Integrating
$\partial(x_i f_t)/\partial x_j$ gives
$H_{ji}=\delta_{ji}J_F-\sum_kQ_{jk}(t)M_{ki}-c_jm_i$.
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 m=-R(\Delta+cJ_F),\qquad
 M=R\bigl(J_F I-cm^{\mathsf T}-H\bigr).
 \label{eq:moments}
\end{equation}
If $i=j$, the boundary value in $H_{ji}$ is the endpoint times a child
master. If $i\ne j$, it is a first moment of a child face, which is reduced
by the same first identity. Each recursive boundary operation fixes another
coordinate, so it eventually terminates at corner values. Keeping these
boundary terms is essential for finite boxes and orthants.

Substitution into the parameter derivative yields
\begin{equation}
 J_F'=-\frac12\tr(E_{AA}M)
       -(E_{AB}b)^{\mathsf T}m
       -\frac12 b^{\mathsf T}E_{BB}b\,J_F .
 \label{eq:derivative}
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:moments} and \eqref{eq:derivative} express every
derivative as a linear combination of face masters. Assembling all faces
therefore gives $J'(t)=\mathcal A(t)J(t)$ with rational coefficients in
$t$. This is the differential system implemented by
\code{BoundarySystem} in \code{transport.py}. The face family provides
a finite closed system; the code does not prove that it is a minimal basis.

\subsection{Dimension and permutation symmetry}

For a general fully bounded box, each coordinate has three face states,
so the system contains $3^d$ masters. With one finite endpoint per
coordinate it contains $2^d$ masters. More generally the count is the
product of the available face-state counts. The default resource cap is
256 masters, checked before enumerating the faces.

When the diagonal entries are equal, the off-diagonal entries are equal,
and all lower bounds and all upper bounds are respectively identical,
coordinate permutations identify equivalent faces. A fully bounded face
then depends only on the numbers of free, lower-fixed, and upper-fixed
coordinates. The master count becomes $\binom{d+2}{2}$. This symmetry
reduction is distinct from the one-dimensional structured formulas derived
below.

\section{What SymPy does}

SymPy is the exact algebra layer~\cite{sympy}. Input decimal strings and
fractions are converted to rational numbers, and the mean is subtracted
from the endpoints before conversion to numerical arithmetic. Ordinary
floating-point inputs retain their supplied decimal representation, not
unknown digits of an underlying physical parameter.

The symbolic construction forms $Q(t)$, inverts its free-coordinate
principal submatrices, and performs the matrix products and traces in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:moments} and \eqref{eq:derivative}. It cancels rational
expressions and extracts numerator and denominator polynomial coefficients
for each nonzero entry of $\mathcal A(t)$. The principal-submatrix
inverses and face-moment identities are reused during construction.
Covariance inversion and the determinant used in the probability
normalization are also exact symbolic operations. If numerical matrix
validation loses a positive pivot through rounding, the code checks
positive definiteness with exact rational arithmetic; diagonal matrices
are checked directly by the signs of their entries.

The Gaussian seeds, error functions, eigenvalue estimates used for step
control, Taylor stepping, and quadrature are numerical operations. SymPy
is not asked to find a symbolic antiderivative of the box integral.
After construction, the numerical engine receives sparse polynomial data,
so symbolic simplification does not occur inside each Taylor step.

\section{Boundary conditions and the BootLoops transport}

\subsection{Exact-form seeds and numerical scaling}

Let $\Phi$ and $\phi$ denote the standard normal distribution function
and density. At $t=0$, free coordinates factor, giving the seed
\begin{equation}
 s_F=J_F(0)=
 \prod_{i\in A}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{D_{ii}}}
       \bigl[\Phi(u_i\sqrt{D_{ii}})-\Phi(\ell_i\sqrt{D_{ii}})\bigr]
 \exp\!\left(-\frac12\sum_{j\in B}D_{jj}b_j^2\right).
 \label{eq:seed}
\end{equation}
These are known univariate formulas, evaluated at the working precision.
The code computes each coordinate's interval mass and endpoint density
once and reuses them across faces.

With $S=\operatorname{diag}(s_F)$ and $W=S^{-1}J$, the initial vector is
$W(0)=\one$ and the connection becomes
$W'=\mathcal C(t)W$, where $\mathcal C=S^{-1}\mathcal A S$.
An entry is multiplied by $s_G/s_F$, not its reciprocal. This scaling
reduces the disparity between initial master magnitudes. After transport,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:probability} becomes
$p=\sqrt{\det Q}\,s_{\varnothing}W_{\varnothing}(1)/(2\pi)^{d/2}$.
The determinant is reused across the higher-precision runs.

\subsection{Taylor continuation along the correlation path}

The numerical call is BootLoops' \code{wayfinder.transport.transport_fixed_eps}.
Its deformation argument is fixed to zero; here that argument has no
dimensional-regularization meaning. About a point $t_0$, write
$\mathcal C(t_0+h)=\sum_{k\geq0}C_kh^k$ and
$W(t_0+h)=\sum_{m\geq0}w_mh^m$. Equating powers gives
\begin{equation}
 w_{m+1}=\frac1{m+1}\sum_{k=0}^{m}C_k w_{m-k} .
 \label{eq:taylor}
\end{equation}
BootLoops advances by local series, checks the decay of the terms, reduces
a step when its numerical tail estimate is too large, and continues to
$t=1$. Its acceptance logic accounts for intervening zero Taylor terms
instead of treating one small term as sufficient evidence of convergence.
The Gaussian adapter supplies the local connection series directly, avoiding
the generic recovery of series coefficients from samples on a complex circle.

The possible finite poles come from $\det Q_{AA}(t)=0$. Factoring out
$D_{AA}$ gives $\det Q_{AA}(t)=\det D_{AA}\det(I+tB_A)$, with
$B_A=D_{AA}^{-1/2}E_{AA}D_{AA}^{-1/2}$. Its eigenvalues are real, and each
nonzero eigenvalue $\lambda$ gives a possible pole $t=-1/\lambda$.
These locations are estimated with arbitrary-precision symmetric
eigenvalue calculations and passed to the engine for step control. Positive
definiteness excludes a pole on the chosen real interval. Some listed poles
can be apparent or cancel in a particular entry.

For a rational connection entry $n(h)/q(h)$, the reciprocal denominator
series starts at $v_0=1/q_0$ and obeys
$v_k=-q_0^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}q_jv_{k-j}$, treating coefficients above
the polynomial degree as zero. Multiplying by the shifted numerator gives
the desired connection series. The implementation in \code{_series.py}
inverts a shared denominator once per expansion point and keeps only
nonzero matrix entries. The local BootLoops adapter accepts this sparse
interface and retains real arithmetic on real paths. Complex waypoint
detours use complex arithmetic.

\subsection{Optional compiled arithmetic}

The Python interfaces retain their existing mathematical definitions.

The default, \code{backend="auto"}, selects \code{python-flint} when
available, and otherwise uses \code{mpmath}. An explicit \code{flint}
selection requires the optional package. The compiled transport uses
BootLoops' \code{AcbFastTable} and its polynomial-convolution Taylor
engine. Gaussian interval kernels also evaluate complementary error
functions in compiled real and complex ball arithmetic~\cite{arb,pythonflint}.

Conversions preserve the stored binary mantissa and exponent instead of
passing through machine-precision numbers. The interval kernels increase
their precision until the local ball resolves the requested working bits.
However, the transport engine returns ball midpoints between accepted
steps; it does not propagate a rigorous enclosure through the entire
calculation. The complete probability therefore remains subject to the
numerical precision and refinement checks described in Section~\ref{sec:accuracy}.
Arithmetic contexts are restored after calls. Concurrent use of the shared
contexts in separate threads is unsupported; separate processes are the
appropriate route for concurrent evaluations.

\section{Exchangeable precision matrices: an auxiliary field}

\subsection{Factorization of the quadratic coupling}

The function \code{exchangeable_precision_probability} treats
$Q=aI+b\one\one^{\mathsf T}$, with $a>0$, $b\geq0$, and common finite
centered bounds $\ell<u$. Its determinant is $a^{d-1}(a+bd)$.
This is a precision-matrix structure: its covariance is
$a^{-1}I-b\one\one^{\mathsf T}/[a(a+bd)]$, so positive $b$ produces
negative off-diagonal covariances. It should not be confused with the
positive-correlation model of the next section.

Put $T_x=\sum_i x_i$. The Gaussian Fourier form of the
Hubbard--Stratonovich transformation~\cite{hubbard} is
$\exp(-bT_x^2/2)=(2\pi b)^{-1/2}\int_{\mathbb R}
\exp[-y^2/(2b)+\mathrm{i}yT_x]\dd y$. It converts the coupling into
a single auxiliary variable and makes the coordinate integrals factor.
Define $I_a(s)=\int_\ell^u\exp(-ax^2/2-sx)\dd x$. Completing the square
expresses it through complementary error functions:
\begin{equation}
 I_a(s)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2a}}\exp\!\left(\frac{s^2}{2a}\right)
 \left[\erfc\!\left(\frac{a\ell+s}{\sqrt{2a}}\right)
      -\erfc\!\left(\frac{au+s}{\sqrt{2a}}\right)\right].
 \label{eq:interval}
\end{equation}
Here $s$ can be complex. Finite bounds make $I_a(s)$ entire, which permits
shifting the auxiliary contour to $y+\mathrm{i}\lambda$ for real
$\lambda$. The Gaussian factor controls the ends of the shifted contour.

\subsection{Saddle centering and the remaining integral}

The shifted representation is
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 p&=\frac{\sqrt{a^{d-1}(a+bd)}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}
       \exp\!\left(\frac{\lambda^2}{2b}\right)I_a(\lambda)^d K_\lambda,\\
 K_\lambda&=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi b}}\int_{\mathbb R}
   e^{-y^2/(2b)}e^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda y/b}
   \left[\frac{I_a(\lambda-\mathrm{i}y)}{I_a(\lambda)}\right]^d\dd y .
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:auxiliary}
\end{equation}
Under the tilted one-coordinate density
$f_\lambda(x)=\exp(-ax^2/2-\lambda x)/I_a(\lambda)$ on $[\ell,u]$,
write $\mu_\lambda=\E_\lambda[X]$ and
$v_\lambda=\Var_\lambda(X)$. The code chooses
$\lambda/b=d\mu_\lambda$, making the linear phase at $y=0$ vanish.
Since $\dd\mu_\lambda/\dd\lambda=-v_\lambda$, the derivative of the
saddle residual is $1/b+dv_\lambda>0$. The root is bracketed between
$bd\ell$ and $bdu$ and found by safeguarded Newton iteration.

The natural width is $w=\sqrt{b/(1+bdv_\lambda)}$. Substituting $y=wz$
centers and scales the integrand. Its conjugate symmetry allows integration
of its real part over $z\geq0$, followed by doubling. The potentially very
small prefactor is accumulated logarithmically. BootLoops'
\code{wayfinder.quad.quad_refine} evaluates the remaining integral using
progressively refined quadrature~\cite{doubleexponential}.

\subsection{A relative bound on the discarded Fourier tail}

For independent draws from $f_\lambda$, let $T=\sum_iX_i$ and
$r_\lambda=d\mu_\lambda-\lambda/b$. Interchanging the expectation and
Gaussian Fourier integral in Eq.~\eqref{eq:auxiliary} gives
$K_\lambda=\E_\lambda\exp[-b(T-\lambda/b)^2/2]>0$.
Jensen's inequality gives
$K_\lambda\geq\exp[-b(dv_\lambda+r_\lambda^2)/2]$.
The bounded-interval variance inequality
$v_\lambda\leq(u-\ell)^2/4$ supplies a computable lower bound.

The absolute value of the characteristic function in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:auxiliary} is at most one. Cutting the auxiliary variable
off at $|y|=Y$ therefore gives the relative discarded-tail bound
\begin{equation}
 \frac{|K_\lambda-K_\lambda^{(|y|\leq Y)}|}{K_\lambda}
 \leq\erfc\!\left(\frac{Y}{\sqrt{2b}}\right)
       \exp\!\left[\frac b2
          \left(\frac{d(u-\ell)^2}{4}+r_\lambda^2\right)\right].
 \label{eq:tail}
\end{equation}
The implementation retains the residual term and increases the cutoff
until this bound meets its target. This controls the omitted tail.
Errors in quadrature, arithmetic, and the prefactor are assessed separately.
For $b=0$, or for one coordinate, the code uses the direct univariate
Gaussian formulas instead of introducing an auxiliary field.

\section{Equal nonnegative correlations: a common Gaussian factor}

The function \code{equicorrelated_probability} treats the representation
$X_i=\mu+\sigma(\sqrt{\rho}\,Z+\sqrt{1-\rho}\,Z_i)$, where all the
standard normal variables are independent and $0\leq\rho<1$.
Standardize the common endpoints by subtracting $\mu$ and dividing by
$\sigma$, and call them $\ell,u$. Conditional independence gives
\begin{equation}
 p=\int_{\mathbb R}\phi(z)g(z)^d\dd z,\qquad
 g(z)=\Phi\!\left(\frac{u-\sqrt{\rho}\,z}{\sqrt{1-\rho}}\right)
      -\Phi\!\left(\frac{\ell-\sqrt{\rho}\,z}{\sqrt{1-\rho}}\right).
 \label{eq:factor}
\end{equation}
This is a positive one-dimensional integral. It supports finite or infinite
equal bounds and a common nonzero mean and standard deviation.

To resolve rare, displaced peaks, define $h(z)=\log\phi(z)+d\log g(z)$.
Let $m_z,v_z$ be the mean and variance of a standard normal restricted to
the conditional interval in Eq.~\eqref{eq:factor}, and put
$r=\sqrt{\rho/(1-\rho)}$. Direct differentiation gives
$h'(z)=-z+drm_z$ and $h''(z)=-1-dr^2(1-v_z)$.
The interval-truncated variance satisfies $0\leq v_z\leq1$, so the log
integrand is strictly concave and has a unique mode. A safeguarded Newton
iteration finds the mode $z_0$. With $w=1/\sqrt{-h''(z_0)}$, the code
integrates $\exp[h(z_0+ws)-h(z_0)]$ and restores the factor
$w\exp[h(z_0)]$ afterward.

The bound $h''\geq-K$, where $K=1+d\rho/(1-\rho)$, also removes the
need for a preliminary integration to estimate the magnitude. For any
chosen center, Taylor's integral remainder bounds
$h(z_0+ws)-h(z_0)$ below by
$h'(z_0)ws-Kw^2s^2/2$. Integrating this lower Gaussian bound shows that
the scaled integral is at least
$\sqrt{2\pi/K}\exp[h'(z_0)^2/(2K)]/w$, hence at least
$\sqrt{2\pi/K}/w$. The code passes half this last quantity as a
conservative tolerance scale to BootLoops quadrature. This argument remains
valid when the numerically found mode has a small nonzero residual.

A useful independent truth is the positive orthant at $\rho=1/2$.
Then $g(z)=\Phi(z)$ and the substitution $v=\Phi(z)$ gives
$p=\int_0^1v^d\dd v=1/(d+1)$. For $d>1$ the structured solver evaluates
the integral; this identity is used for validation, including large
dimensions. At $\rho=0$, or for a single coordinate, it returns through
the direct Gaussian formula.

\section{Precision, logarithms, and numerical evidence}\label{sec:accuracy}

\subsection{Independent formulas and narrow intervals}

For diagonal $Q$, the probability is
$\prod_i[\Phi(u_i\sqrt{Q_{ii}})-\Phi(\ell_i\sqrt{Q_{ii}})]$.
The direct path avoids master allocation, including its exponential resource
cap, and still requires agreement at different working precisions. Full
Gaussian space returns exactly one; a zero-width event returns zero.

Gaussian tails are evaluated with complementary error functions and
reflection, avoiding differences of numbers close to one. A very narrow
finite interval uses a local series instead of subtracting nearly equal
endpoint functions. To see this, write $x=m+vz$ on an interval of midpoint
$m$ and half-width $v$. The exponent becomes a constant plus
$\alpha z+\beta z^2$, where $\alpha=-v(am+s)$ and $\beta=-av^2/2$.
If $\exp(\alpha z+\beta z^2)=\sum_nc_nz^n$, then
$c_0=1$, $c_1=\alpha$, and
$(n+1)c_{n+1}=\alpha c_n+2\beta c_{n-1}$.
Integrating over $-1\leq z\leq1$ uses only the even coefficients,
giving $2\sum_{k\geq0}c_{2k}/(2k+1)$. The same expansion provides
the moments needed in the saddle searches. Exact endpoint differences
determine additional working precision before numerical conversion.

\subsection{Acceptance at increasing precision}

Each numerical route reruns the evaluation with additional working digits;
the quadrature routes also tighten their integration tolerances. For requested
precision $k$, successive probabilities must have relative difference below
$10^{-k}$. For probabilities strictly between zero and one, the code also
compares their logarithms relative to the magnitude of the logarithm.
The general transport additionally checks the residual imaginary component.
Failure to pass the checks raises a convergence error.

When $p$ is near one, an apparently stable rounded value can hide a
meaningful small negative $\log p$. The complement of a box contains every
marginal complement, giving a lower bound on $1-p$. Marginal outside tails
therefore determine extra working digits needed to resolve the logarithm.
The logarithm is computed before the final rounding of $p$, so it can remain
nonzero even if the displayed probability rounds to one.

BootLoops quadrature refines its rule depth until successive estimates
agree within the requested tolerance. Its default tanh--sinh rule is based
on double-exponential quadrature~\cite{doubleexponential}. Local Taylor
tail estimates, quadrature refinement, and higher-precision agreement provide
numerical evidence. The analytic bound in Eq.~\eqref{eq:tail} covers only
the discarded auxiliary tail. These ingredients do not form a rigorous
interval enclosure of the complete probability.

\subsection{Comparison with minimax tilting and the saved receipts}

Botev's minimax tilting method~\cite{botev} constructs a tilted sequential
importance-sampling distribution by solving a saddle optimization. It is a
statistical comparator to the deterministic reductions here. The
one-variable saddle in Eq.~\eqref{eq:auxiliary} is a contour-centering
condition, not Botev's minimax optimization. The project's independent
validation implementation uses twelve scrambled Sobol sequences of 4096
points each, with recorded seeds; those estimator uncertainties are
statistical and do not establish all the candidate's displayed digits.

The saved validation covers the exchangeable-precision Example I in
Section 5.1 at dimensions 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, and 50; the banded-precision
Example II at dimensions 2 and 3; and the exact equal-correlation family
in Section 5.3 at dimensions 10, 100, 1000, and 10000. Example I is also
checked against positive convolution of the original integrand and, at the
smaller dimensions, boundary transport. Example II has an independent
positive product-quadrature check.

In those reports, the positive-convolution relative difference must be below
$10^{-10}$, the Example I boundary comparison below $10^{-(k-2)}$, and
the minimax comparison below the larger of six estimated relative standard
errors and $10^{-10}$. Example II product quadrature requires agreement
below $10^{-12}$ both with the candidate and between its two rule orders.
The exact family requires relative difference below $10^{-k}$. A separate
comparison with the printed paper allows five printed standard errors plus
one last-digit unit and flags discrepancies; it is not the independent
correctness gate.

For Example I at dimension 10, the historical candidate is about
$8.56248967736\times10^{-15}$ whereas the paper prints
$8.556\times10^{-15}$. The receipt preserves this discrepancy alongside
the independent comparisons. These are historical results: the current
compiled kernels, sparse transport, and other performance changes have
not been run. This documentation does not turn the earlier passing checks
into validation of the changed source, and no speedup is claimed.

\section{Reading the implementation and its practical limits}

Table~\ref{tab:source} maps the mathematics to the principal source modules.
General correlated boxes use the boundary route; the two structured
reductions have explicit public interfaces. Recognizing a structured matrix
in the general interface can merge equivalent faces, but does not
automatically select the auxiliary-field or common-factor function.

 \caption{Mathematical responsibilities of the source modules. All paths
 are within the \code{gaussian_orthant} package unless stated otherwise.}
 \label{tab:source}
 \begin{ruledtabular}
 \begin{tabular}{p{0.25\linewidth}p{0.66\linewidth}}
 Module & Responsibility\\
 \code{transport.py} & Exact face reduction, rational connection, independent
 seeds, normalization, and the call to BootLoops transport.\\[3pt]

The general cost is controlled first by the number of faces, then by symbolic
construction and numerical transport. For $s$ nonzero connection entries
and Taylor order $M$, a direct convolution step entails roughly $sM^2$
scalar operations. Sparse storage, shared denominator inverses, and real
arithmetic reduce unnecessary work; compiled polynomial products accelerate
the arithmetic. They do not remove the general exponential growth of the
face family. The structured routes avoid that growth, but still depend on
quadrature and the evaluation of real or complex Gaussian interval functions.
Their applicability is restricted to the matrix families and bounds stated
above. No general large-dimensional performance advantage over minimax
tilting has been established.

The practical outcome is a finite, explicitly derived Gaussian differential
system when the general matrix is small enough, and one-dimensional integrals
for the stated structured families. SymPy establishes the rational algebra;
BootLoops carries out continuation or refined quadrature; precision checks
determine whether a numerical result is accepted. The mathematical identities
explain the algorithm, while fresh numerical validation is still required
for the recently changed implementation.
